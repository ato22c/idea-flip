**Keep the 90-minute slot, but stop making all 10 people listen to everyone's status. Collect status in writing before the meeting, and use the live time only for the few places where two people's work touches.**

🧩 The core conflict: if everyone hears everything, nobody duplicates work but everyone is bored. If we cut the listening, people get bored less but lose track of each other.

### ① Written status, live overlaps only 🟢
- 🛠️ What to do: Before the meeting, each person writes two lines in a shared sheet: "I am working on" and "I might need or touch." In the meeting, read the sheet silently for 10 minutes, then talk only about rows that look alike or collide.
- 🎯 Why it fits: The meeting stays on the calendar, with the same 10 people. It differs from "just send updates by chat" because the meeting time is spent only on collisions, not on reports.
- ⚠️ Where it fails: People skip the sheet or write vague lines like "various tasks."
- 🧪 First experiment: The whole team, 2 weeks. Continue if at least 8 of 10 fill the sheet on time and the team finds at least 1 overlap per week.

---

### ② The weekly matchmaker 🟡
- 🛠️ What to do: One person per week, rotating, reads all 10 lines the day before. They pick 2 or 3 pairs who may be duplicating work and put those pairs on the agenda. Everyone else hears only the pairs that concern them, or works while the pairs talk.
- 🎯 Why it fits: Nobody leaves the meeting or cancels it. The usual answer is "everyone listens to everyone"; here one reader does the matching and the other nine are free from it.
- ⚠️ Where it fails: The matchmaker misses an overlap that is hidden behind different wording.
- 🧪 First experiment: 3 weeks with 3 different matchmakers. Continue if at least 1 real overlap per week is found early, and the team asks the matchmaker's picks "useful" in 2 out of 3 weeks.

---

### ③ Present your partner's work 🟡
- 🛠️ What to do: Pair people up at random. For 10 minutes each pair interviews each other about the week. Then each person explains their partner's work to the whole group in 90 seconds, and the partner corrects mistakes.
- 🎯 Why it fits: Everyone still hears every project, in 5 pairs of short talks. It differs from normal round-robin updates because you listen hard when you must report someone else's work.
- ⚠️ Where it fails: People get anxious about explaining someone else's work badly, and the exercise turns into a performance.
- 🧪 First experiment: 2 weeks. Continue if, in a quick check, at least 7 of 10 can name what 3 colleagues are doing, up from your count today.

---

### 🎲 Wildcard: the meeting is the gate
- 🛠️ What to do: Any new task of more than half a day may only start after it is claimed on a shared board during this meeting. Claiming takes one line. If someone else already holds a similar line, the two talk on the spot.
- 🎯 Why it fits: The meeting is not removed; it becomes the only door to starting work. Duplicates are stopped at the door, so long status reports are no longer needed.
- ⚠️ Where it fails: Urgent work waits for the next meeting and people start quietly outside it.
- 🧪 First experiment: One week, only for new tasks over a day. Continue if no urgent task is blocked more than once and at least 1 clash is caught.

### Next steps
- First check, cheaply, whether the cause is what I guessed: ask the team to write on a sticky note how many of the 90 minutes were useful to them. If most say under 30, go ahead.
- Start ① this week; it needs only a shared sheet.
- After 2 weeks, add ② or ③ if overlaps still slip through.

**Questions to ask the team**
- How could we make this meeting even more boring and useless on purpose, and what is the opposite of that?
- If the meeting could be only 30 minutes long, what would we keep?
- What do we already write down every week (tickets, commits, chat) that nobody reads in the meeting?

### Reasoning
- Goal in plain words: everyone knows enough about others' work to avoid duplication, without everyone sitting through everything.
- Likely cause (a guess): status is read aloud to all 10, yet most of it matters to only 1 or 2 listeners. If the real cause is a bad facilitator or unclear topics, ① still helps but ③ matters less.
- Constraints: the meeting stays, the team stays at 10, duplication must go down.
- ①: splits the work by level, so details live in writing and only collisions are live. Uses an idle resource, the time before the meeting.
- ②: the same split, with one reader standing in for ten listeners. Uses a rotating person as a free resource.
- ③: comes from reversing roles, so listening is forced by the task of reporting. Uses the pairs themselves.
- Wildcard: comes from the extremizing tool of flipping an assumption: the meeting is not for reporting but for permission to start.

### Appendix
- Safe answers set aside: shorter meeting, timed agenda, standing meeting, chat updates, shared wiki, task board, breakout rooms, rotating facilitator.
- Divergent ideas (principle name in brackets, a classic invention rule): [Segmentation (split into parts)] split into pairs; [Prior action (do it beforehand)] written status first; [Intermediary (go-between)] matchmaker; [The other way around] report your partner's work; [Feedback] gate by claiming; [Porosity (empty space)] 10 minutes of silent reading; [Extraction] only collisions go live.
- Extremizing tools used: flipping obvious assumptions (the meeting is for reporting), and doing it backwards (how to make it more boring).
- Set aside 1: A random "pop quiz" where three people must say what a named colleague works on. Too stressful for most teams.
- Set aside 2: Rotating the meeting slot to a walk-and-talk outside. It needs everyone's agreement on the new format and the weather.
