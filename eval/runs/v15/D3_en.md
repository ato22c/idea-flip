Start by moving the plain status updates into a short written board that everyone reads the day before, and keep the meeting only for the parts where people's work touches. The boredom comes from hearing nine updates that mostly don't concern you, and the board removes exactly that.

### ① Read-before board 🟢
- 🛠️ What to do: Every Thursday, each person writes three lines in a shared page: what I finished, what I start next, and who I need. The meeting opens with 10 quiet minutes to read it. Then the team talks only about the lines marked "this touches someone else."
- 🎯 Why it fits: The meeting stays and nobody skips it, so you keep the shared picture. It differs from the common "shorten the meeting" advice because the reporting leaves the room, not just the minutes.
- ⚠️ Where it fails: If people don't write or don't read, the board is empty and the meeting goes back to long updates.
- 🧪 First experiment: The whole team, for 3 weeks. Continue if at least 8 of 10 people have filled in the board before the meeting each week and the meeting ends in 45 minutes or less.

---

### ② Small rooms in parallel 🟡
- 🛠️ What to do: Group people by work area into three groups of three or four. For the first 40 minutes, the groups meet at the same time in separate corners or calls. Then one person per group gives a two-minute summary to everyone.
- 🎯 Why it fits: You keep the same weekly slot with the same people. It differs from the common answer "fewer attendees" because nobody is dropped. The updates run side by side instead of one after another, so each person hears about 3 people instead of 9.
- ⚠️ Where it fails: Two people doing the same work may sit in different groups and never meet. Rotate the groups every month to reduce this.
- 🧪 First experiment: Two meetings in a row. Continue if, afterward, at least 7 of 10 say they heard what they needed, and nobody finds a clash they should have heard about.

---

### ③ "Next week" hunt 🟡
- 🛠️ What to do: Nobody reports the past. Each person puts one sentence on the wall or board: "Next week I will start X." The team spends the meeting hunting for two sentences that overlap or depend on each other, and fixes them on the spot.
- 🎯 Why it fits: The duplicate work you named is caught before it starts, which is cheaper than finding it later. It differs from the common answer "better status reports" because it looks forward and only for overlaps.
- ⚠️ Where it fails: People often don't know what they will start until Monday morning, so the sentences may be vague.
- 🧪 First experiment: The team, for 4 weeks. Continue if the team catches at least 1 real overlap in 4 weeks, and the meeting stays under 60 minutes.

---

### 🎲 Wildcard: one person in the hot seat
- 🛠️ What to do: Each week one person, in rotation, gets 25 minutes: the team asks questions about their work in depth. Everyone else gives a one-line update, nothing more. Over 10 weeks, everyone has been the person in the hot seat once.
- 🎯 Why it fits: Meetings stay weekly with everyone in the room. It differs from the common answer because depth is shared out by turn, instead of everybody getting the same shallow slice.
- ⚠️ Where it fails: The person in the hot seat may feel put on trial, and the other nine may still drift off during the 25 minutes.
- 🧪 First experiment: Two volunteers, two weeks. Continue if at least 7 of 10 people say they learned something new and the volunteers say it felt fair.

### Next steps
1. First, check whether my guess about the cause is right: ask the team in one question, "How much of the meeting concerns your own work?"
2. Also look at the last 3 duplicate-work cases. See whether the overlap would have shown up in a written board.
3. If both fit, start ① next week and add ③ in the second month.

### Reasoning (short)
- Goal: everyone knows what others are doing, so work isn't duplicated, without a boring 90-minute session. I wrote that the meeting must stay, because you said canceling isn't an option.
- Cause (a guess): updates are told one by one to everyone, so most of what each person hears isn't about them. If the real cause is unclear task ownership, the board helps less and ③ helps more.
- Contradiction: the meeting must cover everything (so no one misses anything) and must not cover everything (so no one is bored). This is solved by separation by level: the whole team gets only the shared parts, and details go to a smaller group or a written page.
- ① resolves it by moving the "everything" part into writing. ② resolves it by splitting into small groups. ③ comes from a resource: the meeting time and the people's own plans, used to find overlaps. The wildcard comes from the tool of flipping an assumption ("everyone gets equal time").

### Appendix
Common answers I set aside first: shorter meeting, stricter agenda, standing meeting, status in chat, a project tool dashboard, a timer, no phones, a rotating chair.
Divergent ideas I considered by principle (a principle is one of the classic TRIZ prompts for changing a problem):
- [Segmentation] small parallel groups
- [Prior action] read the updates before the meeting
- [Extraction] only the overlap items remain in the meeting
- [Feedback] the "next week" sentences checked against each other
- [Asymmetry] one person in the hot seat each week
- [Another channel] the update goes into writing or a voice note
- [Partial action] meeting ends the moment the overlaps are done
Two extra tools I used: flipping an assumption ("everyone speaks") and doing it backwards (how to make the meeting worse: let each person talk longer).
