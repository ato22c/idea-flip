**Keep the meeting, but change what it is for: start with a pre-posted list of what everyone will work on, and spend the meeting only on the places where work collides.**

### 📋 At a glance

| Idea | One-line summary | Start |
|---|---|---|
| ① Collision hunt | Post plans the day before, discuss only overlaps | 🟢 start tomorrow |
| ② Pair rounds | Swap updates in rotating pairs, then a short group part | 🟡 within this month |
| ③ Retell your neighbor | Each person reports on someone else's work | 🟡 within this month |
| 🎲 Wildcard: Airtime tokens | The listeners "pay" for the topics they want to hear | 🔴 risky, test small |

---

### ① Collision hunt 🟢
- 🛠️ What to do: By the evening before, each person writes three lines in a shared page: what I start this week, what I need from others, what I just finished. At the meeting, one person reads all ten lists aloud in silence, then the group talks only about lines that overlap or depend on each other.
- 🎯 Why it fits: The meeting stays on the calendar, as you required. It differs from the usual "write status in a doc" answer because the meeting itself is spent hunting for overlaps, not reading updates.
- ⚠️ Where it fails: If people skip writing their lines, or write them too vaguely to show an overlap.
- 🧪 First experiment: The whole team, for 3 weeks. Continue if at least 8 of 10 people post on time and the group finds at least one real overlap in 2 of the 3 weeks.

---

### ② Pair rounds 🟡
- 🛠️ What to do: Split the team into five pairs. Run three rounds of 10 minutes, and change partners each round. Each person tells their partner what they are doing. Then the whole group meets for 20 to 30 minutes, only to share what each pair found.
- 🎯 Why it fits: It keeps the weekly slot and the full team. Instead of one person talking to nine bored listeners, everyone speaks about half the time, which is the opposite of the usual "shorter, stricter agenda" answer.
- ⚠️ Where it fails: Each person hears only three of nine colleagues per week, so an overlap between two people who never meet could stay hidden.
- 🧪 First experiment: Two meetings. Continue if, in a quick vote, at least 7 of 10 say it was less boring, and no duplicated work shows up within a month.

---

### ③ Retell your neighbor 🟡
- 🛠️ What to do: Each person briefly tells what the colleague on their left is working on, based on the colleague's notes. The colleague then corrects it in one sentence.
- 🎯 Why it fits: Listening now has a job, so it is harder to drift off. It differs from a round of self-reports because the speaker is not the owner, which makes gaps in understanding visible.
- ⚠️ Where it fails: If the team fears looking foolish when they get it wrong, they will read the notes word for word.
- 🧪 First experiment: The whole team, for 2 weeks. Continue if the corrections add something new for at least 5 of 10 retellings.

---

### 🎲 Wildcard: Airtime tokens
- 🛠️ What to do: Everyone gets three sticky dots. Before the meeting, people stick dots on the topics they want to hear about. The meeting spends time in the order of dots, and topics with no dots get a one-line mention only.
- 🎯 Why it fits: The audience decides what gets time, so nobody sits through what they do not care about. It is far from the usual "the facilitator sets the agenda."
- ⚠️ Where it fails: Quiet work that nobody asks about gets no airtime, and that is exactly where duplicated work can hide.
- 🧪 First experiment: One meeting on paper. Continue if the meeting ends at least 30 minutes early and no one reports missing an important item afterwards.

### Next steps
Check first whether the cause matches my guess: ask the team which part of the 90 minutes bores them most (for example, "hearing updates that are not about me"). If it is something else, such as unclear leadership or topics, tell me and I will adjust. Then try ① next week, since it needs only a shared page.

### Reasoning
- Problem restated: everyone needs to know what the others are doing, without everyone sitting through everything. Constraint: the meeting must stay, team of 10.
- Likely cause (a guess): updates are broadcast one to ten, so each person hears mostly things that are irrelevant to them. If this is wrong, ideas ② and the Wildcard help less.
- Contradiction: the meeting must be long enough to share everything, yet short enough not to be boring. Resolved by separating by level (what is read on the page vs. what is discussed live) and by condition (only discuss when there is an overlap).
- ① resolves the contradiction by moving plain updates out of the live time. ② and ③ come from the idea of turning listeners into participants. The Wildcard comes from the "start with zero resources" tool: no agenda, only audience votes.

### Appendix
- Safe answers set aside (the "ban list"): shorter meeting, strict agenda, timer, written status in chat or a doc, rotating facilitator, standing meeting, splitting into smaller teams, skipping every other week.
- Divergent ideas (principle names, which are standard problem-solving prompts, are in brackets):
- [Segmentation: cut into parts] pair rounds; topic blocks people may leave.
- [Preliminary action: do it beforehand] posting plans the day before.
- [Inversion: do it backwards] retell your neighbor; the listeners report.
- [Feedback] the neighbor's one-sentence correction.
- [Extremizing: size zero] a meeting with no updates at all, only questions; airtime tokens.
