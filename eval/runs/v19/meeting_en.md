**Start by moving the status updates out of the meeting into a short written board, and use the meeting time only for the places where two people's work overlaps. Reading everyone's update is what makes it boring, and the overlaps are the only part people truly need to hear live.**

### ① Written board, meeting for overlaps only 🟢
- 🛠️ What to do: By Monday noon, everyone writes three lines in one shared page: what I did, what I will do, what I touch. The meeting starts with 10 quiet minutes of reading, then the group discusses only the spots where two people's lines look alike or depend on each other.
- 🎯 Why it fits: The meeting stays on the calendar, as you required. It differs from the common answer "shorten it and keep the round-robin" because nobody reads out their update anymore.
- ⚠️ Where it fails: If people skip writing or skim, the overlaps stay hidden.
- 🧪 First experiment: The whole team for 3 weeks. Continue if at least 8 of 10 people post on time and the meeting ends in under 60 minutes.

---

### ② Overlap hunt 🟡
- 🛠️ What to do: Each person adds 2 or 3 short tags to every task, such as "pricing page" or "customer export". Before the meeting, one rotating person circles tags that appear twice. Those circled items are the agenda.
- 🎯 Why it fits: It attacks your real pain, duplicated work, directly. It differs from the common answer "a better status template" because the agenda is built from matches between people, not from each person's list.
- ⚠️ Where it fails: Two people may describe the same work with different words, so the tags never match.
- 🧪 First experiment: One rotating person, 15 minutes of prep for 4 weeks. Continue if they catch at least 1 real overlap in 3 of the 4 weeks.

---

### ③ Tell my teammate's story 🟡
- 🛠️ What to do: Draw names so that each person gets a teammate's written update and tells it to the group in one minute. The teammate may correct it in 15 seconds.
- 🎯 Why it fits: The meeting still happens and still covers everyone. It differs from the common answer "ask people to pay attention" because listening becomes a job, and a wrong retelling shows right away that the work was not understood.
- ⚠️ Where it fails: Some people may feel exposed if they retell badly, and the team may stop trusting the format.
- 🧪 First experiment: The whole team for 2 meetings. Continue if at least 7 of 10 people say afterwards that they now know what their assigned teammate is working on.

---

### 🎲 Wildcard: Speed-dating rounds
- 🛠️ What to do: Split the 10 people into 5 pairs. Each pair spends 10 minutes explaining their work to each other and writing down any overlap. Then pairs reshuffle, for 3 rounds. Everyone gathers for the last 20 minutes to share only the overlaps found.
- 🎯 Why it fits: It keeps the meeting but removes the long wait to speak. It differs from the common answer "break into small groups once" because the repeated reshuffle spreads information person by person.
- ⚠️ Where it fails: In three rounds, each person meets only three of the nine others, so some overlaps may be missed.
- 🧪 First experiment: One meeting. Continue if the team finds at least 2 overlaps it did not know about and most people rate it less boring than usual.

### Next steps
- This week, ask the team: "Which part of the meeting is boring, and when did we last catch a duplicate there?" This checks my guess about the cause cheaply.
- Start idea ① next Monday and keep the meeting time unchanged for the first 3 weeks.
- If duplicates still slip through, add idea ② on top.

### Reasoning (short)
- Goal: everyone knows what the others are doing, with no duplicated work and no dead time. Cause (my guess): the meeting sends all information to all 10 people, though most of it matters to only one or two. If the real cause is poor trust or unclear task ownership, a better format will help less.
- Contradiction: the meeting must exist to share information, and it must not take 90 minutes of everyone's time. Resolved by separating by level: the whole team sees the written board, and only the affected people talk live.
- Constraint: the meeting cannot be canceled. All four ideas keep it.
- ① moves the information to writing, so the live part drops to the clashes. ② and ③ came from the "people" resource: the team can find overlaps and listen to each other. The wildcard came from flipping the assumption "everyone speaks to everyone."

### Appendix
- Common answers I set aside: shorten the meeting, a strict agenda, a timer per speaker, a shared task board, a chat channel, cutting the team size, a standing meeting.
- Divergent ideas (principle name in brackets; a principle is a standard idea-generating prompt):
  - [Segmentation] split the team into pairs.
  - [Extraction] keep only the overlaps for the live part.
  - [The other way around] retell a teammate's work.
  - [Feedback] a teammate corrects the retelling.
  - [Periodic action] reshuffling rounds.
  - [Prior action] write updates before the meeting.
  - [Blessing in disguise] use the boredom as a signal for what to drop.
  - Extremizing tools (pushing the problem to extremes): a "zero minutes" meeting led to the written board; flipping the assumption "everyone speaks" led to the pair rounds.
